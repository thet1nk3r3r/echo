I’m now working full time with Zobaze.

I also haven’t graduated yet.

I’m still getting used to saying those two things together. There’s more responsibility, more ownership, and somewhere in the background, a BTech degree that would also appreciate my attention.

My degree is loading. Life clicked “continue anyway.”

For most of college, the advice was pretty consistent. Maintain a high CGPA. Practise DSA. Prepare for interviews.

“DSA this, DSA that.”

At some point, I felt like I could build a rocket and someone would still ask whether I could reverse a linked list inside it.

I understood where the advice came from. People wanted me to do well. But I wanted someone to be interested in the things I was actually building too. The problems I was figuring out, the skills I was developing, and the things I stayed up learning even though nobody was going to give me marks for them.

I wanted that part of me to count.

amFOSS gave me a place where it did.

I found people who cared about what I could build and who were just as curious themselves. People who understood why I would spend hours on something that had absolutely nothing to do with the next exam.

Usually because they were doing the same thing.

There were sleepless nights, plenty of frustration, and a lot of “I’ll sleep after this one thing.” A dangerous promise to make when the thing in question has decided to develop a personality.

But I loved figuring things out. I wanted to build something that mattered, even if nobody noticed yet.

And slowly, I started believing in my skills. Every time I worked through something difficult, I had another reason to trust myself the next time I got stuck.

I’ve always wanted to be an entrepreneur too. I didn’t have a perfectly organised plan for it. I knew I wanted to build something useful, take ownership, and see how far I could take it.

That was part of why I reached out to Zobaze. I wanted real work. I wanted to find out what I was capable of when the problems didn’t come with a marking scheme.

I’m really glad I sent that message.

Since then, I’ve worked across different parts of the product—helping improve reliability, working through backend and business workflows, solving problems around inventory and subscriptions, and building tools that help the team get things done.

The work kept giving me reasons to learn something new. I’d start with one problem and realise I needed to understand three other things before I could solve it properly.

My browser tabs became a reasonable visual representation of this process.

And as we worked through these problems, I started thinking more about the work around the work. The repeated steps. The information someone needed before they could start. The things that could be checked automatically. The places where people were spending time moving things along.

That thinking has become a big part of what we do.

We build with agents. We design development workflows around them. We build factories: repeatable ways to take a piece of work through the steps it needs, with agents and tools handling parts of the process and people guiding and reviewing it.

Somewhere along the way, “What can I build?” grew into “How can we build a system that helps us keep building?”

That has been a pretty exciting shift to experience.

I’m still in college, and part of my work is figuring out how to organise development work for AI agents. I occasionally stop and think about how strange and cool that sentence is.

Also, explaining a task clearly to an agent will quickly reveal whether you understood the task yourself.

A humbling little feature.

Working this way has pushed my AI skills much further. I’ve been working with Grok bots, automations, connected tools, and agent-driven development as parts of the same effort: finding better ways to get useful work done.

I’m learning how to break a problem down, give an agent the context it needs, define what a good result looks like, and check what actually happened. I’m getting better at spotting where AI can help and where a person needs to make the call.

There’s a lot of judgment in that. A workflow can look brilliant in your head and then meet its first slightly unusual input.

Suddenly, you have a new task.

But when the pieces start working together, it feels incredible. You can look at something that once needed repeated effort and see a process taking shape around it. You start seeing possibilities that weren’t obvious to you before.

That’s what excites me about where development is heading. As the tools become more capable, I want to keep getting better at deciding what to build, how to organise the work, and how to know whether the result is any good.

I get to develop those skills through actual work, with a team that wants to explore those possibilities too.

And that is a big deal to me.

There are moments where I lean back after working through something and think, “Damn. I actually understand how to do this now.”

Then I nod at the screen as if everything went according to plan.

The laptop and I have agreed to keep the earlier confusion private.

But the satisfaction goes deeper when the work helps someone. When a problem stops getting in the way of their business. When a tool makes a teammate’s day easier. When something we built makes the next piece of work simpler.

That’s where I can feel my mindset changing.

I’m thinking more about users, the business, and how we spend our time. I’m learning to ask whether we’re solving the right problem before getting carried away with the solution. I care more about what happens after something is delivered.

The entrepreneurial mindset I’ve always wanted is becoming something I get to practise.

And a lot of that comes from the people around me.

I found a person and a team who are as hungry as I am.

I don’t think I realised how much that would mean until I experienced it. Having people who understand why you care, who challenge your ideas, and who bring their own ambition into the conversation changes the way you work.

You leave a conversation wanting to get started. You begin to see how much further you could go together.

Their trust has changed me too.

Being given responsibility while you’re still learning makes you want to grow into it. I want to think more carefully, communicate better, and become someone the team can depend on.

I’m still working on the communication part. Apparently, returning after hours of debugging with “fixed” leaves a few gaps in the story.

Fair enough.

But I can see the difference in myself. I take ownership more seriously. I think beyond the immediate task. I’m more willing to ask questions, and more aware of how much I still have to learn.

Now that I’m full time, I feel proud.

I spend so much time looking at what’s next that I sometimes forget to look at how far I’ve come. But the version of me who kept building, who wanted his skills to count, who sent that message because he wanted a chance—I think he’d be happy about this.

He would also immediately ask what we’re building next.

I’m still hungry for more. More learning, more difficult problems, more responsibility, and more things we can build together.

That’s why I say I’m working **with Zobaze**.

I care about what we’re building and where we’re going. I want to contribute ideas, take ownership, and grow alongside the team. I can feel this experience shaping the kind of engineer, teammate, and entrepreneur I want to become.

I’m grateful to amFOSS and to the people who saw something in me beyond my marks. You helped me believe in my skills and gave that curious, restless part of me somewhere to belong.

And I’m grateful I reached out to Zobaze. I went looking for a chance to see what I could do. I found people who have changed how I think and work I want to keep getting better at.

I still have a degree to finish. There’s plenty I haven’t figured out.

But I’m proud of where I am, and I’m excited about who I’m building with.

Turns out, a lot of the education I needed wasn’t in the syllabus.

I should probably still finish the syllabus, though.
